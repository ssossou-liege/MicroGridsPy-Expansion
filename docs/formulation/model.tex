\title{A Multi-Stage Stochastic Capacity-Expansion Model for the Sizing of
Off-Grid Solar Microgrids under Demand, Climate and Economic Uncertainty}
\author{}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
This document develops the mathematical formulation of a capacity-expansion model
for the optimal sizing of off-grid solar microgrids serving rural communities. The
system couples a photovoltaic array, which may be divided between the battery's bus and
the load's bus, a diesel generator, a battery and a hybrid inverter to a community load,
and it may draw on an intermittent national grid. Investment and operation are exposed to
five families of uncertainty---future demand, the renewable resource, economic
conditions, policy and the arrival of the national grid---which are represented by a
discrete scenario tree along whose paths the persistent draws, the community's connection
and growth trajectories and the cost future, are held. The model is posed as an adaptive
multi-stage stochastic program in which capacity is expanded incrementally at successive
milestone years and operation is governed by a rule-based generation-balance controller
rather than by cost minimisation. The controller is simulated hour by hour as the
algorithm it is and is never encoded as constraints; its cost-optimal relaxation, a
mixed-integer linear program of moderate size, serves as the lower bound. We give a
self-contained statement of the sets, parameters, decision variables, constraints and
objective, of the demand model, in which households connect progressively and each cohort
ages on its own clock, and of the construction and reduction of the scenario tree. Beyond
the formulation, we establish that the cost-optimal dispatch problem is a valid lower
bound on the operating cost of the fixed rule-based controller, which lets us certify the
global optimality of the sizing for the controller that is actually deployed and quantify
the ``price of the heuristic dispatch''---a certified gap that, to our knowledge, no
existing off-grid sizing method provides.
\end{abstract}

\tableofcontents

\section{Context}
\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{Background and problem}

Stand-alone (off-grid) solar microgrids---coupling a photovoltaic array, a diesel
generator, battery storage and a hybrid inverter to a community load---are a leading
option for electrifying rural communities beyond the reach of the main grid. Their
economics are dominated by the initial and staged investment in generation and storage
capacity: the sizing decision fixes most of the life-cycle cost and the quality of
service delivered. Sizing is difficult for two reasons. First, the conditions that
determine the value of each asset are deeply uncertain over the multi-decade project
horizon: demand grows as households connect and adopt appliances and productive uses, the
solar resource shifts with the climate, and fuel and equipment prices follow uncertain
trajectories. Second, the cost of a given fleet depends on how it is \emph{operated}, and
in the field operation is governed not by an optimiser but by a fixed, low-cost
rule-based controller embedded in the power electronics. A credible sizing method must
therefore contend with both the uncertainty and the operating policy that will actually
run the plant.

\subsection{Related work}
\label{sec:relwork}

The literature on off-grid microgrid sizing separates into two families distinguished by
how they treat operation and whether they can certify optimality.

The first family relies on \emph{rule-based} dispatch and searches the capacity space
heuristically. The de-facto industry standard, HOMER and HOMER Pro, dispatches the plant
with fixed strategies---load following, cycle charging, generator order, combined and
predictive dispatch---and locates capacities either by enumerating a user-defined grid or
by a proprietary derivative-free optimiser that, in the vendor's own words, can risk
converging to a local optimum. The widely used iHOGA/MHOGA tool of Dufo-L\'opez and
Bernal-Agust\'in optimises both the sizing and the control strategy with genetic
algorithms, returning ``suitable'' rather than certified solutions. Research tools in the
same vein, including the uGrid/uGridNet generation-balance controller adopted in the
present work, likewise pair a rule-based dispatcher with metaheuristic or grid search.
Across this family the operating model is realistic---it is the controller that will run
the plant---but the reported sizing carries \emph{no optimality certificate, gap or
bound}.

The second family uses \emph{exact mathematical programming}. Mixed-integer linear and
non-linear programs co-optimise sizing and dispatch and return a globally optimal (or
bounded) solution for the formulation solved: representative examples are the MILP
planning model of Wu \emph{et al.} for a stand-alone photovoltaic/diesel/battery microgrid
(IEEE Transactions on Smart Grid 12(5), 2021), the two-stage stochastic mixed-integer
programme of Akulker \emph{et al.} (2025), the multi-year MicroGridsPy capacity-expansion
framework of Stevanato \emph{et al.} (Energy for Sustainable Development, 2020), and the
multi-stage stochastic integer template of Ahmed, King and Parija (Journal of Global
Optimization 26, 2003). Decomposition methods can additionally report an optimality gap;
the progressive-hedging scheme in NREL's REopt (Zolan/Wales \emph{et al.}, Optimization
and Engineering 26, 2025) is representative. In every case, however, operation is modelled
as \emph{cost-optimal} economic dispatch: the second-stage decisions are chosen by the
optimiser to minimise short-run cost.

A small number of hybrids sit between the two families but do not close the gap below.
The inner--outer co-optimisation of Lyu \emph{et al.} (Applied Energy 363, 2024) places a
metaheuristic in the outer sizing loop and a cost-optimal energy-management optimiser in
the inner loop, and explicitly deems it ``risky to determine the optimal sizes by
rule-based strategies''. The recent work of Nespoli and Medici (arXiv:2511.21619, 2025)
tunes a rule-based battery controller jointly with its sizing using differential
evolution, but provides no certificate and explicitly dismisses a nested
master/inner-optimisation as too costly; it also addresses grid-connected peak shaving
rather than off-grid supply.

\subsection{Research gap}
\label{sec:gap}

Two observations follow. The methods that use the \emph{deployed} rule-based controller
cannot certify that the chosen sizing is optimal, and typically explore the capacity space
by enumeration or metaheuristics without any bound. The methods that \emph{can} certify
optimality do so for a cost-optimal dispatch that will not run the plant; their optimal
sizing is optimal for the wrong operating model, and the discrepancy---the extra
life-cycle cost incurred because the field controller is heuristic rather than
optimal---is neither certified nor quantified. Consequently:
\begin{quote}
\emph{No published method certifies the global optimality of an off-grid microgrid sizing
for a fixed, non-optimising rule-based dispatch controller, nor quantifies the cost of
using that controller instead of optimal dispatch at the design stage.}
\end{quote}
This is the gap the present work addresses.

\subsection{Hypotheses}
\label{sec:hypotheses}

The approach rests on three hypotheses.
\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.6em,style=nextline]
  \item[H1 (bounding).] For any fixed capacity and scenario, the operating cost under
  cost-optimal dispatch is no greater than the operating cost under the rule-based
  controller, because the controller's trajectory is one feasible dispatch among those the
  cost-optimal problem minimises over. The cost-optimal sizing problem is therefore a valid
  \emph{global lower bound} on the rule-based sizing problem.
  \item[H2 (certification).] Because a computable lower bound (the cost-optimal relaxation)
  can be paired with a computable upper bound (a forward simulation of the actual
  controller), the global optimum of the sizing \emph{for the rule-based controller} can be
  certified by branch-and-bound over the discrete capacity lattice---without ever encoding
  the controller as constraints or approximating its behaviour.
  \item[H3 (price of the heuristic).] The difference between the rule-based and the
  cost-optimal sizing optima is a well-defined, non-negative and computable quantity---the
  \emph{price of the heuristic dispatch}---that measures the value of dispatch optimality at
  the design stage.
\end{description}
Hypothesis~H1 requires only that the controller respect the same physical constraints as
the cost-optimal model; this is stated precisely as Assumption~\ref{ass:feas} and verified
for the generation-balance controller in Section~\ref{sec:certify}.

\subsection{Methodology and contributions}
\label{sec:method}

To fill the gap the document develops, in order:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.8em,itemsep=1pt]
  \item an \emph{adaptive multi-stage stochastic capacity-expansion} model on a scenario
  tree (Sections~\ref{sec:framework}--\ref{sec:objective}) that represents the five
  families of uncertainty---demand, renewable resource, economics, policy and the arrival
  of the national grid---and expands capacity incrementally at milestone years under
  non-anticipativity;
  \item two operating models sharing one feasible set: the rule-based generation-balance
  controller, simulated hour by hour as the algorithm it is (Section~\ref{sec:dispatch}),
  which is the upper-bounding oracle, and its cost-optimal relaxation, which serves as the
  lower-bounding oracle;
  \item the demand model, in which households connect progressively and each cohort ages on
  its own clock, and the construction and reduction of the scenario tree and of the time
  domain (Section~\ref{sec:scenarios}), yielding a tractable deterministic-equivalent
  mixed-integer linear program (Section~\ref{sec:solution});
  \item the central methodological contribution (Section~\ref{sec:certify}): the
  dispatch-relaxation bound of~H1 (Proposition~\ref{prop:bound}), the
  \emph{branch-and-simulate} algorithm that certifies the rule-based sizing optimum
  of~H2 (Corollary~\ref{cor:cert}), and the price of the heuristic dispatch of~H3
  (Definition~\ref{def:price}).
\end{enumerate}
Taken together these bridge the two families of Section~\ref{sec:relwork}: they retain the
\emph{realistic} rule-based controller of the first while delivering the \emph{certified}
optimality of the second, and they make the cost of the heuristic itself a reported
result.

\section{System and modelling framework}
\label{sec:framework}

We consider a stand-alone (off-grid) microgrid whose generation and storage assets
must be sized, and subsequently expanded, so as to supply a growing rural community at
least cost while maintaining an acceptable quality of service. Four technologies are
available. A photovoltaic array provides non-dispatchable renewable generation; a
diesel generator provides dispatchable back-up generation; a
battery provides storage; and a hybrid inverter performs the alternating- and
direct-current conversions that couple these assets to the load. The array may be divided
between the two buses: the part on the battery's bus is admitted by the trackers
integrated in the hybrid inverter, the part on the load's bus by string inverters of its
own (Section~\ref{sec:coupling}). The community load is the only demand. A connection to
an intermittent national grid may already exist or may arrive during the horizon
(Section~\ref{sec:families}); otherwise the plant stands alone.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{system_config.png}
    \caption{microgrid system architecture}
    \label{fig:system_config}
\end{figure}

The planner faces uncertainty that resolves only gradually over the lifetime of the
project. The community's demand grows as households connect, which they do progressively
and in cohorts, as connected households acquire appliances and change their habits, and
as productive activities appear beside them; the solar resource evolves with the climate;
fuel prices and equipment costs follow uncertain trajectories; and the national grid may
arrive. Because the planner can observe how these conditions unfold and react by expanding
capacity at later milestone years, the appropriate decision-making paradigm is
\emph{adaptive multi-stage stochastic programming}. The uncertain future is discretised
into a scenario tree (Section~\ref{sec:tree}); investment decisions are attached to the
nodes of this tree and are therefore allowed to depend on, but only on, the information
revealed up to the stage at which they are taken. This information
restriction---non-anticipativity---is enforced structurally by the tree indexing rather
than by explicit coupling constraints. Part of what is uncertain is a state rather than an
event: a village that connects few of its households goes on connecting few, and a world
in which storage learns quickly goes on learning quickly. Those draws are held along every
path of the tree rather than redrawn at each stage.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{scenario_tree.png}
    \caption{scenario tree for the multi-stage formulation}
    \label{fig:scenarios_tree}
\end{figure}

A distinctive feature of the present model is its treatment of operation. In the
classical capacity-expansion formulation the second-stage operating problem is itself an
optimisation that dispatches each asset to minimise short-run cost. Here we instead
adopt the rule-based ``generation-balance control'' used in off-grid practice, in which
the dispatch follows a fixed priority order---solar first, then the battery, and the
diesel generator only as a last resort---together with a look-ahead rule that reserves
enough stored energy to carry the community through the night. This controller is not
reproduced inside the optimisation. It is simulated hour by hour, as the algorithm it is,
and the cost-optimal operating problem is used only as its relaxation: a lower bound that
the certificate of Section~\ref{sec:certify} pairs with the simulated cost. An earlier
version of this formulation encoded the controller as mixed-integer constraints;
Section~\ref{sec:dispatch} explains why that encoding was withdrawn.

Throughout, the planning horizon is partitioned into stages indexed by milestone years.
In the programme over the tree, each node's year of operation is represented by sixty-four
weighted representative days, each running from sunrise to the next sunrise, through which
the state of charge is chained without interruption; the controller, by contrast, is
always simulated over the full year. The remainder of the document states the model
formally.

\section{Sets and indices}
\label{sec:sets}

The scenario tree is the backbone of the formulation. Its nodes carry both the
investment decisions and the operating sub-problems, and the economics are aggregated
over it.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=2.2cm,style=nextline]
  \item[$\mathcal{N}$] Set of nodes of the scenario tree, indexed by $n$. Each node
  represents one realisation of the uncertain conditions up to its stage. The unique
  root node is denoted $n_0$.
  \item[$\mathcal{G}$] Set of technologies,
  $\mathcal{G}=\{\mathrm{pv},\,\mathrm{pva},\,\mathrm{batt},\,\mathrm{inv},\,\mathrm{ge}\}$,
  denoting respectively the part of the photovoltaic array on the battery's bus, the part
  on the load's bus, the battery, the hybrid inverter and the diesel generator.
  \item[$\mathcal{T}_n$] Set of representative days associated with node $n$, indexed by
  $t$; sixty-four in the programme over the tree. Each representative day stands for a
  cluster of similar days within the operating year that the node represents, and runs
  from sunrise to the next sunrise.
  \item[$\mathcal{H}$] Set of intra-day time steps, indexed by $h\in\{0,\dots,23\}$
  (hourly resolution) and counted from sunrise. Operation is defined on the triples
  $(n,t,h)$. Where the storage recursion steps past the last hour of a representative day
  it continues at the first hour of the next, $(n,t,24)\equiv(n,t+1,0)$.
  \item[$\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{ge}}$] Discrete catalogue of commercially available
  generator ratings, indexed by $s$. The generator is a \emph{single} unit chosen from
  this catalogue and replaced when it is upgraded, whereas the photovoltaic array, the
  battery and the inverter are \emph{modular} and expanded in identical units.
\end{description}

Three structural maps are defined on the tree. For a non-root node $n$, $\pa(n)$ denotes
its parent; $\mathrm{stage}(n)\in\{0,1,\dots,S\}$ denotes the stage (milestone year) at
which $n$ sits; and $\mathcal{L}\subset\mathcal{N}$ denotes the set of leaf nodes, which
correspond to complete scenarios. The path probability $P_n$ of a node is the
probability that the realised scenario passes through $n$; by construction
$\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}}P_\ell=1$.

\section{Parameters}
\label{sec:parameters}

Parameters are grouped into those describing the operating environment of a node, the
technical behaviour of the assets, and the economics. Time-varying environmental
parameters are indexed by $(n,t,h)$ because each tree node carries its own realisation
of demand and resource.

\paragraph{Notation convention.} Throughout the formulation, \emph{uppercase} symbols
denote parameters---data fixed before optimisation---and \emph{lowercase} symbols denote
decision variables, so that the two can be told apart at a glance in every equation. The
exceptions are the policy hyper-parameters $\theta\in\Theta$ of
Section~\ref{sec:refine}, which follow the conventional control-theoretic notation, the
two admission ratios $\rho$ of Section~\ref{sec:coupling}, a handful of named set-valued
maps ($\pa$, $\mathrm{stage}$, $\mathrm{yr}$, $\band$), and the demand model of
Section~\ref{sec:demand}, whose symbols describe how the data $D_{n,t,h}$ are generated
and are introduced there.

\paragraph{Demand and resource.}
\begin{description}[leftmargin=3.0cm,style=nextline]
  \item[$D_{n,t,h}$] Load the plant must produce in time step $(n,t,h)$ [kW]: what the
  customers, the community buildings and the plant's own services draw, increased by the
  energy the distribution network consumes on the way (Section~\ref{sec:demand}).
  \item[$Y_{n,t,h}$] Specific photovoltaic yield, i.e.\ the power produced per unit of
  installed array capacity [kW\,kW$^{-1}$], obtained from the node's irradiance and
  temperature scenario.
  \item[$W_{n,t}$] Weight of representative day $t$ in node $n$ [days\,yr$^{-1}$], equal
  to the number of actual days the representative day stands for; $\sum_{t\in
  \mathcal{T}_n} W_{n,t}=365$.
  \item[{$G^{\mathrm{on}}_{n,t,h}\in\{0,1\}$}] Whether the national grid's feeder is
  energised in time step $(n,t,h)$; zero throughout a node the grid has not reached. The
  outage pattern is generated from the feeder's availability and typical outage length
  rather than copied from one recorded year.
\end{description}

\paragraph{Technical parameters.}
\begin{description}[leftmargin=3.0cm,style=nextline]
  \item[$H^{\mathrm{c}},\ H^{\mathrm{d}}$] One-way charging and discharging
  efficiencies of the battery ($0.975$ each).
  \item[$H^{\mathrm{ac}}$] Efficiency of the double conversion undergone by energy that
  the array on the load's bus sends to storage, up through its string inverter and down
  through the hybrid inverter ($0.94$).
  \item[$A_{n,t,h}$] Fractional self-discharge of the battery over one time step,
  dependent on ambient temperature.
  \item[{$F^{e}_{n,t,h}\in(0,1]$}] Temperature-dependent usable-capacity factor of the
  battery, which scales the upper energy limit; both it and $A_{n,t,h}$ follow the declared
  chemistry, lithium iron phosphate by default.
  \item[$\underline{E},\ \overline{E}$] Lower and upper state-of-charge fractions
  ($0.05$ and $0.95$), expressing the protective trip limits of the battery management
  system.
  \item[$E_0$] State of charge at which operation opens, as a fraction of the upper limit
  ($0.5$).
  \item[$\Gamma$] Largest charging or discharging power per unit of installed energy
  ($0.25$\,h$^{-1}$).
  \item[$R_{n,t,h}$] Look-ahead night-reserve energy [kWh] computed by the
  generation-balance controller to decide when the battery may serve the load
  (Section~\ref{sec:dispatch}). It enters no programme.
  \item[$F^{\mathrm{ge}}$] Minimum stable loading of the generator as a fraction of
  its rating (taken as $0.30$).
  \item[$F_0,\ F_1$] Intercept and slope of a linear \emph{minorant} of the generator's
  fuel consumption [L\,h$^{-1}$ and L\,kWh$^{-1}$], valid across the whole catalogue, so
  that the fuel flow of any unit is at least $F_0+F_1 p^{\mathrm{ge}}$ when it is on.
  \item[$K^{\mathrm{ge}}_s$] Rated power of generator catalogue option $s$ [kW].
  \item[$U^{\mathrm{pv}},\ U^{\mathrm{batt}},\ U^{\mathrm{inv}}$] Unit increments of the
  modular technologies: the rated power of a single photovoltaic panel [kW], on either
  bus, the usable energy of a single battery module [kWh] and the rated power of a single
  inverter module [kW].
  \item[$\rho^{\mathrm{dc}},\ \rho^{\mathrm{ac}}$] Array admitted per unit of hybrid-inverter
  rating on the battery's bus ($1.3$) and on the load's bus ($1.0$)
  (Section~\ref{sec:coupling}).
  \item[$\overline{G}$] Largest power the grid connection can carry [kW], or a bound that
  never binds where none is declared.
\end{description}

\paragraph{Economic parameters.}
\begin{description}[leftmargin=3.0cm,style=nextline]
  \item[$C^{\mathrm{inv}}_{g,n}$] Capital cost of one unit of technology $g$ at the prices
  of node $n$'s cost future [\$\,kW$^{-1}$ or \$\,kWh$^{-1}$].
  \item[$C^{\mathrm{conv}}_n$] Cost of the string inverters per unit of array on the load's
  bus, at node $n$'s prices [\$\,kW$^{-1}$]. Nothing corresponds to it on the battery's
  bus, where the conversion is bought with the hybrid inverter.
  \item[$L_g$] Service life of technology $g$ [yr]: twenty-five years for the array,
  sixteen for the battery, ten for the hybrid inverter, twelve for the string inverters
  and fifteen for the generator.
  \item[$O_{g}$] Annual operation-and-maintenance cost of technology $g$, expressed as a
  fraction of its capital cost.
  \item[$I$] Annual discount rate.
  \item[$\CRF(I,L)$] Capital recovery factor, $I(1+I)^{L}/\big((1+I)^{L}-1\big)$.
  \item[$C^{\mathrm{ann}}_{g,n}$] Annual cost of holding one unit of technology $g$ at
  node $n$, maintenance included; for the array on the load's bus the string inverters are
  added, each part over its own life:
  \begin{gather*}
    C^{\mathrm{ann}}_{g,n}=C^{\mathrm{inv}}_{g,n}\big(\CRF(I,L_g)+O_g\big),\\
    C^{\mathrm{ann}}_{\mathrm{pva},n}
    = C^{\mathrm{inv}}_{\mathrm{pv},n}\big(\CRF(I,L_{\mathrm{pv}})+O_{\mathrm{pv}}\big)
    + C^{\mathrm{conv}}_n\big(\CRF(I,L_{\mathrm{conv}})+O_{\mathrm{conv}}\big).
  \end{gather*}
  \item[$C^{\mathrm{fuel}}_n$] Diesel price at node $n$ [\$\,L$^{-1}$].
  \item[$C^{\mathrm{imp}},\ C^{\mathrm{exp}}$] Price paid for energy imported from the grid
  and price received for energy exported to it [\$\,kWh$^{-1}$].
  \item[$V^{\mathrm{lol}}$] Value of lost load [\$\,kWh$^{-1}$], the penalty applied to
  unmet demand.
  \item[$B_n$] Discount factor that brings costs incurred at node $n$ to present
  value, $B_n=(1+I)^{-\mathrm{yr}(n)}$ where $\mathrm{yr}(n)$ is the calendar year of
  $\mathrm{stage}(n)$.
  \item[$\Delta_n$] Number of operating years that node $n$ represents, i.e.\ the span
  between its stage and the next milestone. The root of a persistent tree stands for none,
  $\Delta_{n_0}=0$: its span is operated by the nodes below it (Section~\ref{sec:tree}).
  \item[$C^{g}_0$] Existing installed capacity of technology $g$ already on site at
  commissioning (brownfield initial condition); zero for a greenfield project. For the
  generator it is the rating $K^{\mathrm{ge}}_{s_0}$ of the unit standing.
\end{description}

\section{Decision variables}
\label{sec:variables}

The decision variables divide naturally into an investment layer, which is attached to
the tree nodes and constitutes the adaptive ``here-and-now'' decisions, and an operating
layer, which is defined on the representative time steps of each node.

\paragraph{Investment layer.} For each node $n$:
\begin{align}
  b^{\mathrm{pv}}_n,\ b^{\mathrm{pva}}_n,\ b^{\mathrm{batt}}_n,\ b^{\mathrm{inv}}_n
    &\in \Zpos
    && \text{modules of array (each bus), battery and inverter added at $n$,}\\
  z^{\mathrm{ge}}_{n,s} &\in \{0,1\}
    && \text{indicator that size $s$ is the single active generator at $n$,}\\
  \mathrm{cap}^{g}_n &\in \Rpos
    && \text{installed capacity of technology $g$ available at $n$.}
\end{align}
The photovoltaic array, battery and inverter are modular: their capacity accumulates
through the integer counts. The generator is a single unit: exactly one catalogue size is
active at each node, $\sum_{s}z^{\mathrm{ge}}_{n,s}=1$, and an upgrade installs a larger
unit in place of the incumbent (Section~\ref{sec:brownfield}).

\paragraph{Operating layer.} For each time step $(n,t,h)$, every source is split by
destination:
\begin{align}
  p^{\mathrm{pv}\to\ell}_{n,t,h},\ p^{\mathrm{pv}\to\mathrm{b}}_{n,t,h},\
  q^{\mathrm{pv}}_{n,t,h} &\in \Rpos
    && \text{battery-bus array: to load, to battery, curtailed [kW],}\\
  p^{\mathrm{pva}\to\ell}_{n,t,h},\ p^{\mathrm{pva}\to\mathrm{b}}_{n,t,h},\
  q^{\mathrm{pva}}_{n,t,h} &\in \Rpos
    && \text{load-bus array: the same [kW],}\\
  p^{\mathrm{ge}}_{n,t,h},\ p^{\mathrm{ge}\to\ell}_{n,t,h},\
  p^{\mathrm{ge}\to\mathrm{b}}_{n,t,h},\ q^{\mathrm{ge}}_{n,t,h} &\in \Rpos
    && \text{generator: output, to load, to battery, spilled [kW],}\\
  p^{\mathrm{d}}_{n,t,h} &\in \Rpos && \text{battery discharge to the load [kW],}\\
  p^{\mathrm{grid}}_{n,t,h},\ p^{\mathrm{exp}}_{n,t,h} &\in \Rpos
    && \text{power imported from and exported to the grid [kW],}\\
  e_{n,t,h} &\in \Rpos && \text{battery energy content (state of charge) [kWh],}\\
  \ell_{n,t,h} &\in \Rpos && \text{unmet demand (loss of load) [kW],}\\
  y_{n,t,h} &\in \{0,1\} && \text{generator commitment (on/off).}
\end{align}
Splitting the sources by destination is what lets the model say which flows cross the
hybrid inverter (Section~\ref{sec:coupling}). Where convenient the charging power is
written
$p^{\mathrm{c}}_{n,t,h}=p^{\mathrm{pv}\to\mathrm{b}}_{n,t,h}+p^{\mathrm{pva}\to\mathrm{b}}_{n,t,h}
+p^{\mathrm{ge}\to\mathrm{b}}_{n,t,h}$. The binary commitment $y_{n,t,h}$ is the only integer
operating variable, one per time step, and the lower bound relaxes it
(Section~\ref{sec:solution}).

\section{Constraints}
\label{sec:constraints}

\subsection{Capacity accumulation and non-anticipativity}
\label{sec:capacity}

The modular technologies accumulate: the capacity available at a node equals the capacity
inherited from its parent plus the modules added at the node,
\begin{align}
  \mathrm{cap}^{\mathrm{pv}}_n   &= \mathrm{cap}^{\mathrm{pv}}_{\pa(n)}
      + U^{\mathrm{pv}}\, b^{\mathrm{pv}}_n, \label{eq:cap-pv}\\
  \mathrm{cap}^{\mathrm{pva}}_n  &= \mathrm{cap}^{\mathrm{pva}}_{\pa(n)}
      + U^{\mathrm{pv}}\, b^{\mathrm{pva}}_n, \label{eq:cap-pva}\\
  \mathrm{cap}^{\mathrm{batt}}_n &= \mathrm{cap}^{\mathrm{batt}}_{\pa(n)}
      + U^{\mathrm{batt}}\, b^{\mathrm{batt}}_n, \label{eq:cap-batt}\\
  \mathrm{cap}^{\mathrm{inv}}_n  &= \mathrm{cap}^{\mathrm{inv}}_{\pa(n)}
      + U^{\mathrm{inv}}\, b^{\mathrm{inv}}_n. \label{eq:cap-inv}
\end{align}
The generator, by contrast, is a single active unit whose rating is set by the catalogue
state,
\begin{align}
  \mathrm{cap}^{\mathrm{ge}}_n &= \sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{ge}}}
      K^{\mathrm{ge}}_s\, z^{\mathrm{ge}}_{n,s},
  \qquad \sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{ge}}} z^{\mathrm{ge}}_{n,s}=1,
  \label{eq:cap-ge}\\
  \mathrm{cap}^{\mathrm{ge}}_n &\geq \mathrm{cap}^{\mathrm{ge}}_{\pa(n)},
  \label{eq:ge-monotone}
\end{align}
where~\eqref{eq:ge-monotone} restricts the generator to upgrades (its rating never
decreases along the tree). A node that holds its parent's plant, which the operating-only
nodes below a persistent root do (Section~\ref{sec:tree}), adds nothing to it:
$b^{g}_n=0$ for the modular technologies and $z^{\mathrm{ge}}_{n,s}=z^{\mathrm{ge}}_{\pa(n),s}$
for every size. The parent state of the root encodes the initial condition,
$\mathrm{cap}^{g}_{\pa(n_0)}=C^{g}_0$ for the modular technologies and
$\mathrm{cap}^{\mathrm{ge}}_{\pa(n_0)}=K^{\mathrm{ge}}_{s_0}$ for the incumbent generator
(all zero for a greenfield project; Section~\ref{sec:brownfield}). Because every scenario
that shares a common history passes through the same node, and the investment variables
are indexed by node rather than by scenario, non-anticipativity is enforced implicitly by
these recursions: two scenarios cannot be assigned different capacities before the stage
at which they diverge. No additional coupling constraints are required.

\subsection{Brownfield expansion and generator replacement}
\label{sec:brownfield}

The formulation covers both greenfield projects and---the common case in
practice---\emph{brownfield} projects that expand an already commissioned microgrid. On
the supply side the two differ only in the root's initial condition. A greenfield project
starts empty, $C^{g}_0=0$; a brownfield project seeds the root's parent with the as-built
fleet, $\mathrm{cap}^{g}_{\pa(n_0)}=C^{g}_0>0$ for the modular technologies and the
incumbent rating $K^{\mathrm{ge}}_{s_0}$ for the generator. The same expansion machinery
then applies unchanged. On the demand side a brownfield site also starts from what is
there: the households already connected, each with its own connection date
(Section~\ref{sec:demand}).

Two economic facts follow. First, the plan pays for what it adds and for nothing else: the
capital term of Section~\ref{sec:objective} charges the capacity the plan has bought,
$\mathrm{cap}^{g}_n-C^{g}_0$, and for the generator the rating chosen less the rating
standing. The standing plant is not free---its own replacements fall due---but what it
still owes is the annuity of those replacements deferred by the age of each vintage, a
constant that does not depend on the decision. It shifts every cost alike and is left out
of the minimisation, moving neither the optimum nor the certificate. Second, the standing
plant still operates: every dispatch constraint uses the cumulative
$\mathrm{cap}^{g}_n$, which includes $C^{g}_0$, and so does the array-to-converter ceiling
of Section~\ref{sec:coupling}, a converter having to admit the array already on its
trackers as well as the one the plan buys.

Because the modular technologies only ever add modules, their capacity is monotone
non-decreasing and no retirement is modelled. The generator is different: it is a single
lumpy unit, so expansion means \emph{replacing} the incumbent with a larger catalogue size
rather than running several units at part load. The state
formulation~\eqref{eq:cap-ge}--\eqref{eq:ge-monotone} captures exactly this. A rating
below the one standing is excluded at every node, since a plant does not sell its
generating set back, and an upgrade carries no resale credit for the unit it replaces:
under the annuity accounting of Section~\ref{sec:objective} the incumbent's charge simply
stops when the larger unit's begins.

None of this affects the certificate of Section~\ref{sec:certify}: the capacity lattice is
merely offset so that its lower corner is the existing fleet $C^{g}_0$, rounded down to
whole modules so that a plate which is not a whole number of modules never hands the plan
capacity nobody installed, and the constant owed on the standing plant cancels in both
$z_A^\star$ and $z_B^\star$, leaving the price of the heuristic dispatch
$\Delta_{\mathrm{heur}}$ unchanged in structure.

\subsection{Power balance and the national grid}
\label{sec:balance}

Every kilowatt an array produces goes to the load, to the battery, or nowhere,
\begin{equation}
  p^{\mathrm{pv}\to\ell}_{n,t,h}+p^{\mathrm{pv}\to\mathrm{b}}_{n,t,h}+q^{\mathrm{pv}}_{n,t,h}
  = Y_{n,t,h}\,\mathrm{cap}^{\mathrm{pv}}_n,
  \qquad
  p^{\mathrm{pva}\to\ell}_{n,t,h}+p^{\mathrm{pva}\to\mathrm{b}}_{n,t,h}+q^{\mathrm{pva}}_{n,t,h}
  = Y_{n,t,h}\,\mathrm{cap}^{\mathrm{pva}}_n,
  \label{eq:pv-split}
\end{equation}
and in each operating time step the supply reaching the load must equal the served demand,
\begin{equation}
  p^{\mathrm{pv}\to\ell}_{n,t,h} + p^{\mathrm{pva}\to\ell}_{n,t,h}
  + p^{\mathrm{ge}\to\ell}_{n,t,h} + p^{\mathrm{d}}_{n,t,h} + p^{\mathrm{grid}}_{n,t,h}
  = D_{n,t,h} - \ell_{n,t,h},
  \qquad \forall (n,t,h).
  \label{eq:balance}
\end{equation}
The product $Y_{n,t,h}\,\mathrm{cap}^{g}_n$ couples a parameter to a decision variable and
is therefore linear. The battery's charging appears in the split of each source rather than
as a demand on the balance; unmet demand $\ell_{n,t,h}$ relaxes the balance at the cost of
the value of lost load.

Where the national grid has reached the village, it may serve the load and absorb what the
arrays would otherwise curtail, within the hours its feeder is energised:
\begin{equation}
  p^{\mathrm{grid}}_{n,t,h} \le G^{\mathrm{on}}_{n,t,h}\,\overline{G},
  \qquad
  p^{\mathrm{exp}}_{n,t,h} \le G^{\mathrm{on}}_{n,t,h}\,\overline{G},
  \qquad
  p^{\mathrm{exp}}_{n,t,h} \le q^{\mathrm{pv}}_{n,t,h}+q^{\mathrm{pva}}_{n,t,h},
  \qquad \forall (n,t,h).
  \label{eq:grid}
\end{equation}
A node the grid has not reached has $G^{\mathrm{on}}=0$ throughout, which removes both
flows. The grid belongs in the programme as well as in the simulation: a relaxation unable
to import would price the plant above what a controller that can import achieves, and it
would cease to be a lower bound.

\subsection{Generator operation}
\label{sec:generator}

The generator's output is split between the load, the battery and a spill,
\begin{equation}
  p^{\mathrm{ge}\to\ell}_{n,t,h}+p^{\mathrm{ge}\to\mathrm{b}}_{n,t,h}+q^{\mathrm{ge}}_{n,t,h}
  = p^{\mathrm{ge}}_{n,t,h},
  \label{eq:ge-split}
\end{equation}
the spill being what the unit must produce beyond what the load and the battery take in
order to stay above its minimum loading. The generator may produce power only when
committed, must respect its installed rating, and---when running---must operate above its
minimum stable loading. With $M$ a sufficiently large constant (conveniently the largest
catalogue rating),
\begin{align}
  p^{\mathrm{ge}}_{n,t,h} &\leq \mathrm{cap}^{\mathrm{ge}}_n, \label{eq:ge-cap}\\
  p^{\mathrm{ge}}_{n,t,h} &\leq M\, y_{n,t,h}, \label{eq:ge-on}\\
  p^{\mathrm{ge}}_{n,t,h} &\geq F^{\mathrm{ge}}
      \Big(\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{ge}}}K^{\mathrm{ge}}_s\,
      z^{\mathrm{ge}}_{n,s}\Big) - M\,(1-y_{n,t,h}). \label{eq:ge-min}
\end{align}
Constraint~\eqref{eq:ge-on} forces the output to zero when the unit is off; together with
\eqref{eq:ge-min} it imposes the minimum loading $F^{\mathrm{ge}}$ of the single active
generator when the unit is on, and is vacuous when it is off. Because the generator is one
unit, the minimum loading applies to that unit alone---this is precisely the efficiency a
single, rightly-sized generator provides over several modules idling at part load. The
right-hand side reuses the state variables $z^{\mathrm{ge}}_{n,s}$ of the capacity
definition, and since these are per-node design variables (not per-time-step), no new
per-time-step integer or bilinear term is introduced.

\subsection{Battery storage dynamics}
\label{sec:battery}

The battery energy content evolves from one time step to the next according to a charge
balance that accounts for conversion losses and self-discharge,
\begin{equation}
  e_{n,t,h+1} = (1-A_{n,t,h})\,e_{n,t,h}
  + H^{\mathrm{c}}\big(p^{\mathrm{pv}\to\mathrm{b}}_{n,t,h}
  + H^{\mathrm{ac}}\,p^{\mathrm{pva}\to\mathrm{b}}_{n,t,h}
  + p^{\mathrm{ge}\to\mathrm{b}}_{n,t,h}\big)
  - \frac{p^{\mathrm{d}}_{n,t,h}}{H^{\mathrm{d}}},
  \qquad \forall (n,t,h),
  \label{eq:soc-dynamics}
\end{equation}
with $(n,t,24)\equiv(n,t+1,0)$, so that the recursion runs through a node's
representative days one after the other, and through the hours of the year in order where
a full year is solved. It opens where the controller opens,
\begin{equation}
  e_{n,1,0} = E_0\,\overline{E}\,\mathrm{cap}^{\mathrm{batt}}_n,
  \qquad \forall n,
  \label{eq:soc-opening}
\end{equation}
and it ends wherever the dispatch leaves it: no closing condition is imposed, neither on
each day nor on the year. Both alternatives were tried, and both spoil the bound, the first
by loosening it and the second by invalidating it.

Splitting the year into self-contained days, each opening at the same state of charge,
hands the programme a free recharge every morning: three hundred and sixty-five a year,
worth about a sixth of the annualised cost on the reference site, which puts the bound
below anything achievable. Closing the recursion instead---requiring the energy content to
return to its opening level at the end of a day or of the year---constrains the programme
where the controller is not constrained. The relaxation of Proposition~\ref{prop:bound}
minorises the cost of a trajectory produced by the rule-based controller, and no rule
requires the stored energy to return to its opening level at any horizon: a year that ends
on a cloudy week ends with a depleted pack. Imposing a closure on the minorant therefore
constrains it away from the trajectory it is meant to bound, and the bound loses its
validity. This is not hypothetical---imposed over a week of the reference site, the closure
raised the ``lower'' bound some fifteen per cent above the rule trajectory's own cost,
inverting the inequality. A free terminal state relaxes every terminal condition and so
remains a valid minorant of any of them.

Running each representative day from sunrise rather than from midnight is what makes the
compression faithful to the night. The calendar day cuts the night in half and leaves the
shorter half at its end: on the reference site the evening peak falls at nineteen hours,
four hours before the array stops producing, while the night it opens lasts thirteen. A
day that starts at sunrise holds the whole night it has to carry, and rolling the year to
the hour the array starts producing halves the error a compressed year makes on the
operating cost.

The stored energy is bounded below by the protective discharge trip and above by the
temperature-derated usable capacity, and the charging and discharging powers by the
battery's admissible rate,
\begin{equation}
  \underline{E}\,\mathrm{cap}^{\mathrm{batt}}_n
  \;\leq\; e_{n,t,h} \;\leq\;
  \overline{E}\, F^{e}_{n,t,h}\,\mathrm{cap}^{\mathrm{batt}}_n,
  \qquad \forall (n,t,h),
  \label{eq:soc-bounds}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  p^{\mathrm{c}}_{n,t,h} \le \Gamma\,\mathrm{cap}^{\mathrm{batt}}_n,
  \qquad
  p^{\mathrm{d}}_{n,t,h} \le \Gamma\,\mathrm{cap}^{\mathrm{batt}}_n,
  \qquad \forall (n,t,h).
  \label{eq:batt-rate}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Coupling architecture}
\label{sec:coupling}

What the hybrid inverter has to carry is not a property of the plant alone but of where the
array is joined to it, and the arrangements in use differ both in physics and in price. The
array may be divided between two buses. The part on the battery's bus,
$\mathrm{cap}^{\mathrm{pv}}_n$, is admitted by the maximum-power-point trackers integrated
in the hybrid inverter and reaches storage directly; whatever it sends to the load crosses
the inverter. The part on the load's bus, $\mathrm{cap}^{\mathrm{pva}}_n$, feeds the load
through string inverters of its own without crossing the hybrid inverter, but whatever it
sends to storage is converted twice, which $H^{\mathrm{ac}}$ charges
in~\eqref{eq:soc-dynamics}. The generator stands on the load's bus, so its charging current
crosses the inverter too. Hence
\begin{equation}
  p^{\mathrm{pv}\to\ell}_{n,t,h} + p^{\mathrm{d}}_{n,t,h}
  + p^{\mathrm{ge}\to\mathrm{b}}_{n,t,h} + p^{\mathrm{pva}\to\mathrm{b}}_{n,t,h}
  \;\leq\; \mathrm{cap}^{\mathrm{inv}}_n,
  \qquad \forall (n,t,h).
  \label{eq:inv-bounds}
\end{equation}

The array is not free of the converter that admits it. A hybrid inverter's integrated
trackers accept a bounded array per unit of rating --- current three-phase units publish
thirty-nine kilowatts of array for thirty of inverter --- and an array on the load's bus is
held at parity, since off grid whatever the load does not take must be absorbable by the
battery inverter the instant the load drops. Hence
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{cap}^{\mathrm{pv}}_n \;\leq\; \rho^{\mathrm{dc}}\, \mathrm{cap}^{\mathrm{inv}}_n,
  \qquad
  \mathrm{cap}^{\mathrm{pva}}_n \;\leq\; \rho^{\mathrm{ac}}\, \mathrm{cap}^{\mathrm{inv}}_n,
  \qquad \forall n,
  \label{eq:array-ratio}
\end{equation}
with $\rho^{\mathrm{dc}}=1.3$ and $\rho^{\mathrm{ac}}=1.0$, the whole field reaching at most
the sum of the two ceilings. Constraint~\eqref{eq:array-ratio} is what makes enlarging the
array and enlarging the converter one decision rather than two, and it is not a refinement:
on the reference instance it removes some forty-seven per cent of the Cartesian lattice as
plants that cannot be wired. Nothing in the model sizes charge controllers outside the
hybrid inverter. Two regulators on one lithium bank must answer to the same
battery-management conversation if they are not to negotiate the charge current against
each other, which confines the arrangement to a single manufacturer's ecosystem; the
conversion on the battery's bus is therefore bought with the inverter, and only the string
inverters of the load's bus appear as a separate cost, $C^{\mathrm{conv}}_n$.

The two pure arrangements are the corners of this division. Direct-current coupling is
$\mathrm{cap}^{\mathrm{pva}}_n=0$; alternating-current coupling is
$\mathrm{cap}^{\mathrm{pv}}_n=0$, under which~\eqref{eq:inv-bounds} reduces to everything
entering or leaving storage crossing the inverter. Searching over the division therefore
holds both, and settles in one search what certifying each arrangement over its own lattice,
as an earlier version of this work did, settled in two. On the single certified year the
division need not even be searched. The battery-bus part costs less, having no string
inverters to buy, and it reaches storage without a second conversion; it competes with the
discharge for the inverter only when the inverter is congested in daylight, which the
certified trajectories never are. Filling the battery's bus to its ceiling and placing the
remainder of the field on the load's bus is therefore the division the search would choose,
and deriving it removes a dimension from the lattice; the rule is validated against the
undivided search rather than asserted.

\subsection{Rule-based generation-balance control}
\label{sec:dispatch}

The operating policy of the microgrid is not cost minimisation but the rule-based
generation-balance controller used in off-grid practice. Its priority order is
straightforward: photovoltaic power serves the load first; any surplus charges the
battery and is curtailed only once the battery is full; remaining demand is met from the
battery; and the generator is started only when the battery alone cannot carry the load.
A look-ahead rule refines this priority at night: the battery is allowed to serve the
load only if it holds enough energy to last until the next photovoltaic surplus, and
otherwise the generator is started and used both to serve the load and to recharge the
battery up to---but no further than---the level required for the night. The quantity that
rule compares the stored energy with is the night reserve
\begin{equation}
  R_{n,t,h} \;=\; M^{\mathrm{r}}\,\hat R_{n,t,h},
  \label{eq:night-reserve}
\end{equation}
where $\hat R_{n,t,h}$ is the forecast net demand over the next $L$ hours, until the next
photovoltaic surplus, clipped to the installed energy capacity. The deployed setting is a
reserve multiplier $M^{\mathrm{r}}=1$, a look-ahead of $L=14$ hours and a generator
setpoint equal to the minimum stable loading, one point $\theta_0$ of the parameterised
class of Section~\ref{sec:refine}. The controller acts under the physics of this section:
the same trip limits, temperature-derated ceiling and self-discharge, the same minimum
stable loading and conversion limits, and a part-load fuel curve of which
$F_0+F_1p^{\mathrm{ge}}$ is a minorant; it never charges and discharges in the same hour.

The controller is \emph{simulated}, hour by hour over the whole year, as the algorithm it
is. An earlier version of this formulation proposed instead to impose the reserve as a
hard floor on the stored energy, $e_{n,t,h}\ge R_{n,t,h}$, in the cost-optimal programme,
and asserted that the resulting programme, with a single commitment binary per hour,
reproduces the field controller. It does not, on two counts, and the distinction turns
out to be the whole subject of this work rather than a detail of encoding.

First, the floor is not satisfied by the controller it is supposed to describe. The
reserve governs whether the battery is \emph{permitted to serve the load}; it does not
prevent the battery being drawn down when the generator is saturated or absent, in which
case the controller discharges to the protective trip rather than shed load. Measured on
the reference site, the rule trajectory sits below $R_{n,t,h}$ in between four and sixty
per cent of the hours depending on how the plant is sized, by as much as seventy-five
kilowatt-hours. Imposed as a constraint, the floor would therefore exclude the very
behaviour it claims to encode, and a bound computed under it could exceed the trajectory
it is meant to minorise --- the same failure as the closure of the storage recursion in
Section~\ref{sec:battery}, and for the same reason.

Second, and more fundamentally, a programme is not a controller. Adding the floor to the
cost objective yields an \emph{anticipative} optimisation: it minimises over the whole
horizon knowing the whole horizon. The field controller is \emph{causal}, deciding hour by
hour with a bounded look-ahead and no knowledge of the weather beyond it. The distance
between the two is exactly $\Delta_{\mathrm{heur}}$, the quantity this work sets out to
measure, so encoding the rule as a programme would measure the cost of the reserve
constraint in place of the cost of myopia.

The operating layer is therefore represented twice, and the two representations are of
different kinds. The \emph{lower} oracle is the programme
\eqref{eq:pv-split}--\eqref{eq:array-ratio} under the cost objective alone, whose optimum
$C^{\mathrm{opt}}_n(x)$ is computable and minorises the controller by
Proposition~\ref{prop:bound}. The \emph{upper} oracle is the controller itself, executed
step by step as the algorithm it is; no formulation of it is used, and none is needed.
Equation~\eqref{eq:night-reserve} defines a quantity the controller computes; it is imposed
on no programme.

A softened form of the floor --- required only in the hours when the generator is not
already at its rating, which is precisely when the controller does respect it --- would
give a valid and strictly tighter lower bound than the cost objective alone. That is
recorded here as an opening rather than a result.

\subsection{Curtailment and unmet demand}
\label{sec:slacks}

Curtailment and spill are non-negative and bounded by the splits~\eqref{eq:pv-split}
and~\eqref{eq:ge-split}, and unmet demand cannot exceed the demand itself,
\begin{equation}
  q^{\mathrm{pv}}_{n,t,h},\ q^{\mathrm{pva}}_{n,t,h},\ q^{\mathrm{ge}}_{n,t,h} \ge 0,
  \qquad
  0 \leq \ell_{n,t,h} \leq D_{n,t,h},
  \qquad \forall (n,t,h).
  \label{eq:slacks}
\end{equation}

\section{Objective}
\label{sec:objective}

The planner minimises the expected discounted total cost over the scenario tree. Capital is
not paid as a lump sum at installation: every node-year is charged the annual cost of
holding the capacity the plan has bought by then, each asset over its own service life.
Writing the capacity the plan holds at node $n$ as
\begin{equation}
  x^{g}_n = \mathrm{cap}^{g}_n - C^{g}_0
  \quad (g\in\{\mathrm{pv},\mathrm{pva},\mathrm{batt},\mathrm{inv}\}),
  \qquad
  x^{\mathrm{ge}}_n = \sum_{s} \big(K^{\mathrm{ge}}_s-K^{\mathrm{ge}}_{s_0}\big)^{+}
  z^{\mathrm{ge}}_{n,s},
\end{equation}
the annual capital charge of the node is
\begin{equation}
  A_n \;=\; \sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}} C^{\mathrm{ann}}_{g,n}\, x^{g}_n,
  \label{eq:capital}
\end{equation}
the operating cost of one representative year is
\begin{equation}
  G_n \;=\; \sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_n} W_{n,t} \sum_{h\in\mathcal{H}}
  \Big[
    C^{\mathrm{fuel}}_n\big(F_0\,y_{n,t,h} + F_1\,p^{\mathrm{ge}}_{n,t,h}\big)
    + V^{\mathrm{lol}}\,\ell_{n,t,h}
    + C^{\mathrm{imp}}\,p^{\mathrm{grid}}_{n,t,h}
    - C^{\mathrm{exp}}\,p^{\mathrm{exp}}_{n,t,h}
  \Big],
  \label{eq:operating}
\end{equation}
and the objective is
\begin{equation}
  \min \quad
  \sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}} P_n\,B_n\,\Delta_n
  \big( A_n + G_n \big).
  \label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}

The annuity $C^{\mathrm{ann}}_{g,n}=C^{\mathrm{inv}}_{g,n}\big(\CRF(I,L_g)+O_g\big)$ is the
equivalent annual cost of holding one unit of $g$ and replacing it at the end of every service life, for
ever, at the prices of the node's cost future, maintenance included. Charging it at every
node-year for the capacity held has three consequences. Each asset is recovered over its
own life rather than the horizon's: a ten-year inverter charged at a twenty-five-year factor
would look two fifths cheaper than it is, which is precisely the bias that produces
oversized converters. No replacement schedule has to be tracked down the tree. And no
salvage value enters: the horizon does not truncate an asset, whose annuity simply ceases
to be charged when the horizon ends. Pre-existing capacity is excluded from $x^{g}_n$
(Section~\ref{sec:brownfield}).

The battery carries no throughput charge. Its capital is recovered over its calendar life,
and charging its wear per kilowatt-hour discharged as well would pay for the same pack
twice. Nor is a cycle-life limit worth carrying in the programme. The wear of a pack
overtakes its calendar charge only when it discharges more than
$\big(\CRF(I,L_{\mathrm{batt}})+O_{\mathrm{batt}}\big)\,N^{\mathrm{cyc}}\,
(\overline{E}-\underline{E}) = 0.1730\times6000\times0.90\approx934$ kWh a year per
kilowatt-hour installed, $N^{\mathrm{cyc}}$ being its cycle life, which is 2.84 full cycles
every day of the year. A solar plant charges once a day, and every pack certified in this
work discharges about 272 kWh a year per kilowatt-hour, under a third of the threshold. The
calendar life is what ends the pack.

The path probabilities $P_n$ weight each node by the likelihood of its history, so that
\eqref{eq:objective} is the expectation of the discounted life-cycle cost over the
scenario tree---a risk-neutral criterion. The levelised cost of electricity is reported
from the same annual charges.

\section{Uncertainty space, demand model and scenario tree}
\label{sec:scenarios}

\subsection{The families of uncertainty}
\label{sec:families}

The uncertain future is described by five families of random factors, each evolving along
the planning horizon.
\begin{description}[leftmargin=2.4cm,style=nextline]
  \item[Demand] The community load is generated bottom-up, from the connection of
  households, the behaviour of the connected and the productive activities beside them
  (Section~\ref{sec:demand}). Two of its draws are states of the community and are held
  over a path: the \emph{connection trajectory}, which says how many households connect
  and how fast, and the \emph{growth trajectory}, which says how the consumption of a
  connected household evolves with the age of its connection. The rest---the behaviour each
  connected household adopts in a month, how its appliances are used, how many enterprises
  have opened---is redrawn for every simulated year and carries the inter-annual
  variability.
  \item[Renewable resource] The solar resource follows one of the shared
  socio-economic pathways SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5 and SSP3-7.0, each of which yields
  a distinct trajectory of irradiance and ambient temperature and hence of the specific
  yield $Y$. The high-forcing pathway SSP5-8.5 is deliberately excluded: its emission
  trajectory is now widely regarded as implausible rather than as a business-as-usual
  baseline, and retaining it would place probability mass on a future that inflates the
  spread of the resource scenarios without informing the sizing decision.
  \item[Economic conditions] The capital costs of the array, the battery, the inverter and
  the generator and the diesel price follow one of three cost futures---low, central and
  high---each a set of real annual rates per technology, with a break after ten years
  beyond which learning is expected to slow. The future is drawn once per path and held: a
  world in which storage learns quickly is one in which converters do too. Fuel does not
  learn, and its band is centred on a flat real price, an admission of ignorance rather
  than a forecast.
  \item[Policy] A minimum solar-penetration target can be sampled as a discrete factor per
  stage. It is carried by the scenario paths but imposes no constraint in the present
  model.
  \item[National grid] Where the arrival of the national grid is uncertain, each milestone
  carries the probability that the line arrives during it, given that it has not arrived
  before. The arrival is drawn once per path as the milestone at which it occurs, and it is
  absorbing: once the line is there it stays, and from then on its availability, its
  outages and its tariffs enter operation through $G^{\mathrm{on}}$
  (Section~\ref{sec:balance}).
\end{description}

\subsection{Demand model}
\label{sec:demand}

The load $D_{n,t,h}$ of a node is a draw from a stochastic generator calibrated on the meter
records and household surveys of two reference villages, Samionta and Gbowele. It is built
in layers, each estimated on what was measured and none extrapolated beyond it.

\paragraph{Behavioural archetypes.} The household-months of the meter records are segmented
into four behavioural archetypes, and into two states that are not behaviours:
\emph{inactive}, a connected household that consumes nothing in the month, and
\emph{outlier}, the residue of observations excluded from the segmentation, whose small
mass is folded back into the archetypes in proportion. Each archetype carries a set of
appliances---ratings, counts, durations, windows of use---calibrated on the surveys and
corrected by one power factor and one duration factor so as to reproduce the archetype's
measured daily energy and peak. A household in an archetype is simulated as one user of
the RAMP appliance-usage generator at minute resolution, and the community is aggregated to
hourly power. A residual calendar-month index restores the seasonality that survives once
the age of the connection is accounted for.

\paragraph{A mixture law conditioned on maturity.} Which state a connected household
occupies in a month is drawn from a mixture law conditioned on its socio-economic category
$k\in\mathcal{K}$ and on the maturity band $\beta$ of its own connection---the months since
it was connected, in the bands 0--3, 4--6, 7--12, 13--24 and 25 or more---rather than on the
village or the calendar month. Conditioning on the village does not transfer: predicting
either reference village from the other errs by 50 and by 130 per cent on daily energy. The
two villages agree to within a few per cent at connection and diverge afterwards, so that
the level transfers to a new site and the trajectory does not. The law is estimated with
the two sites weighted equally, each stratum being shrunk towards the pooled law of its band
in proportion to its own support.

\paragraph{Growth trajectories.} With two villages, one that grows and one that hardly does,
the growth of a third cannot be estimated, and the two observed behaviours are carried as an
envelope rather than averaged into a trajectory that describes neither. The growth
trajectory $\gamma=\mathrm{slow}$ is Samionta's law, $\gamma=\mathrm{fast}$ is Gbowele's,
and $\gamma=\mathrm{central}$ is the site-balanced law; we write $\pi_\gamma(c\mid k,\beta)$
for the probability of state $c$. A band in which the trajectory's village has no record
inherits the last band it does have, which holds growth constant rather than inventing its
continuation: no household grows beyond the terminal band.

\paragraph{Progressive connection.} Households do not connect all at once. Measured in months
$a$ since the village's first connection, the connected share of the census follows a
logistic diffusion with plateau $S_\omega\in(0,1]$, rate $\Lambda_\omega>0$ and midpoint
$A^{\mathrm{mid}}_\omega$,
\begin{equation}
  \lambda_\omega(a) = \frac{1}{1+\exp\!\big(-\Lambda_\omega\,(a-A^{\mathrm{mid}}_\omega)\big)},
  \qquad
  N_k(a) = \Big\lfloor S_\omega\,\lambda_\omega(a)\,H_k + \tfrac12 \Big\rfloor,
  \label{eq:connection}
\end{equation}
where $H_k$ is the census of category $k$ and $N_k(a)$ the households of that category
connected by month $a$; rounding each category separately keeps every $N_k$ non-decreasing.
The connection trajectory $\omega\in\{\mathrm{low},\mathrm{central},\mathrm{high}\}$ is
bracketed by the two villages, as growth is: $\mathrm{low}$ is the curve fitted to
Samionta's connected households, $\mathrm{high}$ the curve fitted to Gbowele's, and
$\mathrm{central}$ lies between them. Samionta stopped connecting after a first wave, its
active households levelling off near a fifth of the census; Gbowele's are still rising two
years after its plant was commissioned, so that its record bounds its plateau from below
and not from above, and the plateau of the high trajectory is a convention rather than a
measurement, at whose admissible ends the sizing is reported. The curves are fitted to the connected households, whether or not they
consume: a connected household that consumes nothing is the inactive state of the mixture
law, which must not be counted twice. The model in which the whole census is connected at
once is the degenerate case $S_\omega=1$, $\Lambda_\omega\to\infty$,
$A^{\mathrm{mid}}_\omega=0$.

\paragraph{Cohorts.} The households connected in month $u$ form a cohort of
$n_k(u)=N_k(u)-N_k(u-1)$, and each cohort ages on its own clock: at village age $a$ its
members are $a-u$ months old, in band $\band(a-u)$. The mixture law therefore applies cohort
by cohort, not to the community as a whole. Grouping the connected households by the band of
their cohort, the composition of the month at village age $a$ is
\begin{equation}
  X(a) = \sum_{\beta}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}} X_{k,\beta}(a),
  \qquad
  X_{k,\beta}(a) \sim \operatorname{Mult}\Big(\sum_{u\,:\,\band(a-u)=\beta} n_k(u),\;
  \pi_\gamma(\cdot\mid k,\beta)\Big),
  \label{eq:composition}
\end{equation}
independent multinomial draws under one law adding up to a single draw. Unconnected
households are in no state and draw nothing. A community whose households keep connecting
therefore keeps changing long after its first cohort has matured, and a village whose
connection has reached its plateau settles once its last cohort reaches the terminal band.

\paragraph{Productive uses.} Enterprises are counted rather than composed. Six activity
classes---refrigeration, milling, incubation, tailoring, sawmill and small trade---carry
their measured mean day and its day-to-day spread, since a mill that runs hard two days in
three is not a mill running steadily at two thirds. The number of enterprises in a month is
\begin{equation}
  U(a) \sim \operatorname{Poisson}\Big(\Phi_\gamma\big(\band(a)\big)
  \sum_{c\neq\mathrm{inactive}} X_c(a)\Big),
\end{equation}
an intensity referred to the connected households, measured at the reference villages and
conditioned on the village's maturity and on the growth trajectory, the classes being then
allocated multinomially from the trajectory's own mix. Enterprises thereby follow the
connected households: an enterprise appears beside existing supply, and a ratio taken over
dwellings not yet connected would not transfer. A surveyed count or roster of enterprises
replaces the expectation or the draw.

\paragraph{Community uses and the plant's own services.} Places of worship, measured at the
reference villages, and community buildings a developer describes appliance by appliance are
present from the first month, being among the first things a line is run for. So is what
the plant serves for itself: the air conditioning of the room the inverters stand in, at the
plant, and the street lighting, out on the network. Neither is billed to anyone.

\paragraph{From consumption to the load the plant produces.} The distribution network
consumes a share $F^{\mathrm{net}}$ of the energy injected into it, so the plant must inject
$D^{\mathrm{net}}/(1-F^{\mathrm{net}})$ to deliver $D^{\mathrm{net}}$ to the loads out on the
network, while its own supply is drawn from the busbar beside it and carries no loss:
\begin{equation}
  D = \frac{D^{\mathrm{net}}}{1-F^{\mathrm{net}}} + D^{\mathrm{plant}}.
  \label{eq:network-loss}
\end{equation}
The share is $F^{\mathrm{net}}=0.13$, measured on the distribution meters of Gbowele. A later
measurement at both villages, in October 2026, found an unexplained gap of eighteen to
nineteen per cent between what the plants delivered and what the metered loads account for,
and a residual that does not follow the hourly load; both the value and the proportional
form are therefore under review.

\paragraph{An existing village.} A brownfield site starts from its record: the households
connected by the record's last month $a^{\mathrm{obs}}$, $N^{\mathrm{obs}}_k$ of each
category, each in the cohort of its own connection month. The connection trajectory
continues from there,
\begin{equation}
  \begin{gathered}
  N_k(a) = N^{\mathrm{obs}}_k + \Big\lfloor \big(\overline{N}_k - N^{\mathrm{obs}}_k\big)
  \frac{\lambda_\omega(a)-\lambda_\omega(a^{\mathrm{obs}})}{1-\lambda_\omega(a^{\mathrm{obs}})}
  + \tfrac12 \Big\rfloor,
  \qquad a > a^{\mathrm{obs}},\\
  \overline{N}_k = \max\Big(\Big\lfloor S_\omega H_k + \tfrac12\Big\rfloor,\,
  N^{\mathrm{obs}}_k\Big),
  \end{gathered}
  \label{eq:connection-brownfield}
\end{equation}
which begins on the observation, never decreases and never disconnects anyone. The age of
the village at the plan's first year then follows from the record rather than being
declared.

\subsection{Monte-Carlo sampling, scenario reduction and tree construction}
\label{sec:tree}

A large Monte-Carlo ensemble of complete scenario paths is drawn. Each path draws once, and
holds over the horizon, its \emph{regime} $\xi=(\omega,\gamma,\kappa)$---its connection
trajectory, its growth trajectory and its cost future---and, where the grid's arrival is
uncertain, the milestone at which the line arrives. At every milestone it draws the climate
pathway and a realisation of the community's year, which carry the inter-annual
variability. Given the regime and the age of the village the generator's draws are
exchangeable, so the realisations are taken from small cached pools sampled with
replacement, which represent the conditional distribution as faithfully as a fresh
simulation per path, at a hundredth of the cost. Because the resulting ensemble is far too
large to optimise over directly, it is condensed in two complementary ways.

First, the ensemble is reduced \emph{across scenarios}, and the reduction respects the
distinction just drawn. The root of the tree is the here-and-now decision and holds a single
node. The regime is revealed at the first milestone after it: the tree branches there over
regimes, and every node below a regime's branch keeps that regime, so that along every path
of the tree the connected households never decrease, the growth trajectory never changes and
the prices follow one future. When the ensemble's regimes outnumber the branches allowed,
they are reduced to medoid regimes, each a regime that occurs and each carrying the
probability mass of its cluster. Below each regime, the stage-wise outcomes of that regime's
own paths are clustered---by $k$-medoids on what a sizing is sensitive to: the energy to
serve, its peak and when in the day it falls, the resource, how the two line up, and the
prices---and the representatives are nested as a fan, so that climate and inter-annual
variability branch freely within the regime. The arrival of the grid is absorbing: below a
node the line has reached, every node is connected. The path probability $P_n$ is the
product of the conditional branch probabilities along the path. A two-stage program is
recovered as the special case of a tree with a single decision node at the first stage.

The root's own span is operated under every regime. The regime is revealed at the first
milestone, but the year-0 plant runs the years before it under whichever regime is true, and
a root holding one year of data, the medoid of the whole ensemble, would never see the
spread of the span in which a new village connects most of its households. The root
therefore carries the investment and no operation, $\Delta_{n_0}=0$, and below it sits one
operating-only node per regime branch, at the root's milestone and for the root's span, with
the branch's probability, the medoid of that regime's own outcomes at year~0, and the root's
plant, to which it adds nothing (Section~\ref{sec:capacity}). The first-milestone nodes of
the regime hang below it. In node-indexed form this is the two-stage structure of the year-0
decision: one plant, operated under every regime.

A node is charged $\Delta_n$ years of operation on one year of data, and which year of its
span that is matters once households connect progressively, the first year of a span being
its least demanding. The data are those of the span's first year or, as an option, of its
last; only the demand differs between the two, the yield, the temperature and the prices
being the milestone's in either case.

Revealing the regime at the first milestone is an assumption, and it is stated as one: five
years of operation show how many households a village connects, how their consumption grows
and which way prices are moving, to within the bracket the regimes span. The persistence is
not an assumption but a correction. An earlier construction nested the stage-wise medoids of
the whole ensemble as a fan, every node of a stage receiving the same children, and along a
path of that tree a village could grow quickly to its fifth year, shrink by its tenth and grow
again---a mean reversion that neither the reference villages nor the learning literature
support.

The distortion the reduction introduces is measured and reported rather than assumed small:
at every stage, the mean distance of an outcome to the node standing for it; across regimes,
the distance of each regime to its representative; and over whole trajectories, both the
distance from each sampled path to the nearest path of the tree and the distance from each
path of the tree, weighted by its probability, to the nearest sampled path. The last is what
a fan nesting fails: it contains every combination of stage representatives, and so gives
weight to trajectories that no village follows.

Second, the ensemble is reduced \emph{in time}. Within each node, the year of operation is
compressed into sixty-four weighted representative days by clustering the daily shapes of
demand and resource together, each day running from sunrise to sunrise; the medoid of each
cluster becomes a representative day $t$ with weight $W_{n,t}$ equal to the size of its
cluster. Medoids are real days, so the compressed year keeps genuine peaks and genuine calm
spells rather than the flattened average a centroid would produce. The compression is
imposed on the programme alone, where on the reference site it costs about 0.6 per cent of
the bound and buys a twelvefold speed-up. It is never imposed on the simulation of the
controller, which runs the whole year at every node: the quantity the simulation measures
is the cost of not knowing the coming days, and a set of representative days, each simulated
on its own, distorts precisely that.

\section{Deterministic equivalent and solution}
\label{sec:solution}

Collecting the node-indexed investment variables, the time-step-indexed operating
variables, the constraints of Section~\ref{sec:constraints} over all nodes and time
steps, and the objective~\eqref{eq:objective} yields a single mixed-integer linear
program---the deterministic equivalent of the multi-stage stochastic program under
cost-optimal operation. Its integer variables are the module counts $b^{\mathrm{pv}}_n$,
$b^{\mathrm{pva}}_n$, $b^{\mathrm{batt}}_n$ and $b^{\mathrm{inv}}_n$, the generator state
$z^{\mathrm{ge}}_{n,s}$, and one generator-commitment variable per operating time step,
which the lower bound relaxes to $[0,1]$: the relaxation keeps the bound valid and makes it
far cheaper. On the reference tree of forty-six nodes and sixty-four representative days the
programme has some seven hundred and eighty thousand columns. The non-anticipativity of the
investment policy is, as noted, embedded in the tree indexing and requires no explicit
constraints. The model is implemented in an algebraic modelling layer over labelled
multidimensional arrays and solved by an open-source branch-and-cut solver, or by a
commercial one where a licence is available, which returns the same bound in about half the
time. When the programme is solved with integer variables, the value used as a lower bound
is the solver's guaranteed dual bound and not its incumbent, which bounds the programme's own
optimum from above.

Two scopes are used, and they differ in what they guarantee. The single certified year of
Section~\ref{sec:certify} is solved at one node over the full year of 8\,760 hours: the
cost-optimal programme is then a true relaxation of the controller, and its value a valid
bound. On the tree, the programme is solved on representative days, which is an
approximation rather than a relaxation: the weighted cost of the representative days is
neither above nor below that of the full year in general. Its deviation from the full-year
programme is measured---about 0.6 per cent of the bound at sixty-four days aligned on
sunrise on the reference site---and is reported with every result that rests on it.

\section{Certified-optimal sizing under the rule-based dispatch policy}
\label{sec:certify}

The deterministic equivalent of Section~\ref{sec:solution} returns a globally optimal
sizing \emph{under the assumption that operation is cost-optimal}. In off-grid practice,
however, the controller that will actually be deployed is the rule-based
generation-balance policy of Section~\ref{sec:dispatch}, which is in general
sub-optimal. The sizing that is optimal for the deployed controller therefore differs
from the sizing that is optimal for cost-minimising dispatch, and it is the former that a
planner ultimately needs. This section shows that one can certify global optimality of
the sizing \emph{for the fixed rule-based controller}, without encoding the controller as
constraints, by using the cost-optimal dispatch problem as a rigorous lower bound and the
forward simulation of the controller as an upper bound.

\subsection{Two operating cost functions}

Let $x$ denote the complete vector of investment decisions---equivalently the cumulative
capacities $\{\mathrm{cap}^{g}_n\}_{g,n}$---and let $\mathcal{X}$ be the finite discrete
lattice of admissible investments (integer array, battery and inverter module counts within
bounds---floored at the existing fleet $C^{g}_0$ and cleared of the plants the
ceilings~\eqref{eq:array-ratio} cannot wire---and the single generator's catalogue size).
For a fixed $x$ and a node $n$, write $\mathcal{F}_n(x)$ for the operating feasible set
defined by the splits and the balance~\eqref{eq:pv-split}--\eqref{eq:grid}, the generator
constraints~\eqref{eq:ge-split}--\eqref{eq:ge-min}, the storage
dynamics and limits~\eqref{eq:soc-dynamics}--\eqref{eq:batt-rate}, the conversion
limit~\eqref{eq:inv-bounds} and the slacks~\eqref{eq:slacks}, and let $c_n(\cdot)$ be the
per-year operating cost $G_n$. Define
\begin{align}
  C^{\mathrm{opt}}_n(x) &= \min_{u\,\in\,\mathcal{F}_n(x)} c_n(u)
      && \text{(cost-optimal dispatch),}\\
  C^{\mathrm{rule}}_n(x) &= c_n\big(u^{\mathrm{rule}}_n(x)\big)
      && \text{(rule-based dispatch),}
\end{align}
where $u^{\mathrm{rule}}_n(x)$ is the trajectory produced by the generation-balance
controller. With $A_n$ as in Section~\ref{sec:objective}, the two sizing objectives are
\begin{equation}
  Z_\bullet(x) = \sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}} P_n\,B_n\,\Delta_n
  \big( A_n(x) + C^{\bullet}_n(x) \big),
  \qquad \bullet\in\{\mathrm{opt},\mathrm{rule}\},
\end{equation}
with global optima $z_A^\star=\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}} Z_{\mathrm{opt}}(x)$ (the
deterministic equivalent of Section~\ref{sec:solution}) and
$z_B^\star=\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}} Z_{\mathrm{rule}}(x)$ (the quantity of interest).

\subsection{The dispatch-relaxation lower bound}

\begin{assumption}[Policy feasibility]
\label{ass:feas}
For every $x\in\mathcal{X}$ and node $n$, the rule-based trajectory lies in the operating
feasible set, $u^{\mathrm{rule}}_n(x)\in\mathcal{F}_n(x)$.
\end{assumption}

Assumption~\ref{ass:feas} merely states that the controller respects the same physics as
the cost-optimal model. It holds for the generation-balance controller of
Section~\ref{sec:dispatch}: the controller satisfies the power balance with the unmet-load
slack, keeps the state of charge within the trip limits~\eqref{eq:soc-bounds}, runs the
generator above its minimum loading or not at all~\eqref{eq:ge-min}, respects the inverter
limits~\eqref{eq:inv-bounds}, and never charges and discharges simultaneously; its
trajectory is thus one feasible point of $\mathcal{F}_n(x)$, not necessarily the
cost-minimising one.

\begin{proposition}[Dispatch-relaxation bound]
\label{prop:bound}
Under Assumption~\ref{ass:feas}, $C^{\mathrm{opt}}_n(x)\le C^{\mathrm{rule}}_n(x)$ for all
$x\in\mathcal{X}$ and all $n$. Consequently $Z_{\mathrm{opt}}(x)\le Z_{\mathrm{rule}}(x)$
for every $x$, and $z_A^\star\le z_B^\star$. Moreover, for any box
$B\subseteq\operatorname{conv}(\mathcal{X})$, the continuous-capacity relaxation
$\underline{Z}(B)=\min_{x\in B}Z_{\mathrm{opt}}(x)$ is a valid lower bound on
$\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}\cap B} Z_{\mathrm{rule}}(x)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By Assumption~\ref{ass:feas}, $u^{\mathrm{rule}}_n(x)$ is feasible for the minimisation
defining $C^{\mathrm{opt}}_n(x)$, hence
$C^{\mathrm{opt}}_n(x)=\min_{u\in\mathcal{F}_n(x)}c_n(u)\le
c_n(u^{\mathrm{rule}}_n(x))=C^{\mathrm{rule}}_n(x)$. The capital terms $A_n$ are identical
in $Z_{\mathrm{opt}}$ and $Z_{\mathrm{rule}}$, and the weights
$P_n B_n\Delta_n\ge0$; summing preserves the inequality, giving
$Z_{\mathrm{opt}}(x)\le Z_{\mathrm{rule}}(x)$ pointwise and $z_A^\star\le z_B^\star$ after
minimisation. For the relaxation, any $x\in\mathcal{X}\cap B$ satisfies
$Z_{\mathrm{rule}}(x)\ge Z_{\mathrm{opt}}(x)\ge\min_{x'\in B}Z_{\mathrm{opt}}(x')
=\underline{Z}(B)$, since $\mathcal{X}\cap B\subseteq B$ and integrality is relaxed.
\end{proof}

The bound needs no monotonicity argument: relaxing integrality (a superset of capacities)
and replacing the rule-based cost by the cost-optimal cost (a smaller value) both can only
\emph{decrease} the objective, so $\underline{Z}(B)$ underestimates the rule-based optimum
over the box. Nor does it need the programme's fuel curve to be the controller's: the
programme prices fuel with the minorant $F_0+F_1p^{\mathrm{ge}}$, valid across the
catalogue, while the simulation prices it with the installed unit's own part-load curve, and
evaluating the rule trajectory with the minorant can only lower its cost, so the inequality
holds \emph{a fortiori}. The lower-bounding problem $\underline{Z}(B)$ is exactly the
deterministic-equivalent program of Section~\ref{sec:solution} restricted to $x\in B$;
relaxing the generator-commitment binary in it yields a still-valid but cheaper linear
lower bound.

\subsection{Branch-and-simulate}

Proposition~\ref{prop:bound} pairs a computable lower bound (solve the cost-optimal
relaxation on a box) with a computable upper bound (forward-simulate the controller at an
integer point), which is exactly what a branch-and-bound certificate requires.

\begin{corollary}[Certificate]
\label{cor:cert}
For any incumbent $\hat{x}\in\mathcal{X}$ and any partition $\{B_k\}$ of
$\operatorname{conv}(\mathcal{X})$,
$\min_k \underline{Z}(B_k)\le z_B^\star\le Z_{\mathrm{rule}}(\hat{x})$. If
$Z_{\mathrm{rule}}(\hat{x})-\min_k\underline{Z}(B_k)\le\varepsilon$, then $\hat{x}$ is an
$\varepsilon$-optimal sizing for the rule-based controller.
\end{corollary}

This gives the following procedure, which we call \emph{branch-and-simulate}.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em,itemsep=1pt]
  \item Initialise the incumbent value $\bar{z}\leftarrow+\infty$ and a queue of open
  boxes with the root $B_0=\operatorname{conv}(\mathcal{X})$.
  \item Pop a box $B$. Compute the lower bound $\underline{Z}(B)$ by solving the
  cost-optimal relaxation (Section~\ref{sec:solution}) restricted to $x\in B$.
  \item If $\underline{Z}(B)\ge\bar{z}$, \emph{prune} $B$
  (Proposition~\ref{prop:bound}).
  \item Otherwise round the relaxed solution to an integer point $\hat{x}\in
  \mathcal{X}\cap B$, evaluate the upper bound $Z_{\mathrm{rule}}(\hat{x})$ by
  \emph{forward-simulating} the controller on every node and scenario, and update the
  incumbent if it improves.
  \item Branch: split $B$ along an integer capacity coordinate and return the children to
  the queue; a box holding few enough designs is instead enumerated by simulation.
  \item Terminate when the queue is empty, every design of $\mathcal{X}$ having been either
  discarded by a bound or evaluated by a simulation; return the incumbent.
\end{enumerate}
Because $\mathcal{X}$ is finite the branching terminates, and on termination the incumbent
is a certified global optimum of the rule-based sizing problem $z_B^\star$. Termination is
by exhaustion and not by a vanishing gap, and cannot be otherwise: as boxes shrink, the
bound of a singleton is exactly the cost-optimal value at that design, so the smallest bound
over the queue tends to $z_A^\star$ while the incumbent tends to $z_B^\star$, and their
difference tends to $\Delta_{\mathrm{heur}}>0$. What the bound buys is not a closing gap but
the right to discard whole regions unexamined. Only linear or mixed-integer relaxations
(lower bounds) and forward simulations of the real controller (upper bounds) are ever
solved; the controller is never approximated by constraints, so its behaviour is reproduced
\emph{exactly}. The pruning is tight precisely when the rule-based controller is close to
cost-optimal, so that the method is fast in the regime where the heuristic is good and
merely slower---never incorrect---when it is poor.

The two oracles do not cost the same, and on real data the ratio dictates the order of the
work. A year of simulated operation costs about a millisecond once the hourly loops are
compiled, a relaxation over the full year tens of seconds. The search therefore buys a strong
incumbent first, by a coarse multi-resolution sweep of simulations, and spends relaxations
only on proving that nothing better remains, a relaxation being worth its price only on a box
holding many more designs than one relaxation costs simulations.

On the tree, the object to certify is the root and not the plan. Only the year-0 plant is
bought; everything below it is a future the plan is ready for. The same scheme applies with
boxes taken in root coordinates alone. The lower oracle is the deterministic equivalent with
the root confined to the box and every descendant left free, which by
Proposition~\ref{prop:bound} minorises the rule-based cost of every plan rooted in that box,
to within the compression error of Section~\ref{sec:solution}; the upper oracle is a
coordinated descent over the plan with the root pinned, which returns a buildable plan and
therefore a true cost. When a box narrows to a single plant, the descent bounds the best plan
rooted there from above only, so a plant whose bound still lies below the incumbent cannot be
excluded until the descent is tight enough; such plants are reported rather than rounded away.

\subsection{The price of the heuristic dispatch}

\begin{definition}[Price of the heuristic dispatch]
\label{def:price}
$\;\Delta_{\mathrm{heur}} = z_B^\star - z_A^\star \ge 0$, reported in absolute terms and as
the relative gap $\Delta_{\mathrm{heur}}/z_A^\star$.
\end{definition}

By Proposition~\ref{prop:bound} this quantity is non-negative; it measures the avoidable
life-cycle cost attributable to operating the microgrid with the rule-based controller
instead of an ideal cost-optimal dispatcher, evaluated at each method's own optimal
sizing. It is a by-product of the same two solves---$z_A^\star$ is the
deterministic-equivalent optimum and $z_B^\star$ the branch-and-simulate optimum---and is
itself an informative result: a small $\Delta_{\mathrm{heur}}$ justifies the operational
simplicity of the heuristic, whereas a large one quantifies the value of investing in
smarter control.

\subsection{Refining the controller: a parameterised rule class}
\label{sec:refine}

The generation-balance controller of Section~\ref{sec:dispatch} is one point in a much
larger space of deployable, causal operating policies. Rather than commit to a single set
of rules, we embed it in a \emph{parameterised policy class}
$\{\pi_\theta : \theta\in\Theta\}$, where each $\pi_\theta$ is a causal controller that,
at every time step, maps the current battery energy and the information available up to
that step to a feasible dispatch. Four parameters capture the levers that matter most for
off-grid operating cost.
\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.9em,style=nextline]
  \item[Commitment hysteresis $(\tilde E_{\mathrm{on}},\tilde E_{\mathrm{off}})$.] With
  $\underline{E}\le\tilde E_{\mathrm{on}}\le\tilde E_{\mathrm{off}}\le\overline E$, the
  generator starts when the state of charge would fall below
  $\tilde E_{\mathrm{on}}F^{e}\,\mathrm{cap}^{\mathrm{batt}}$ and, once started, keeps
  running until the charge reaches $\tilde E_{\mathrm{off}}F^{e}\,\mathrm{cap}^{\mathrm{batt}}$.
  A wide band suppresses short, inefficient generator cycles.
  \item[{Generator setpoint $T^{\mathrm{ge}}\in[F^{\mathrm{ge}},1]$.}] When the
  generator runs it targets a loading $T^{\mathrm{ge}}$ of its rating; the power in
  excess of the served deficit charges the battery. $T^{\mathrm{ge}}=F^{\mathrm{ge}}$
  recovers pure load-following, while larger values realise cycle-charging that runs the
  unit near its efficient point.
  \item[Reserve multiplier $M^{\mathrm{r}}\ge0$.] The night-reserve floor is scaled,
  $R_{n,t,h}(\theta)=M^{\mathrm{r}}\,\hat R_{n,t,h}$, where $\hat R_{n,t,h}$ is the forecast net
  demand until the next photovoltaic surplus; $M^{\mathrm{r}}$ trades reliability against fuel and
  subsumes the fixed reserve of Section~\ref{sec:dispatch} at a nominal $M^{\mathrm{r}}_0$.
  \item[Look-ahead horizon $L\ge0$.] The number of future steps the policy anticipates
  through a forecast of demand and yield; $L=0$ is purely reactive and increasing $L$
  yields predictive (receding-horizon) control.
\end{description}
Named strategies are special cases: load-following is
$T^{\mathrm{ge}}=F^{\mathrm{ge}}$ with $\tilde E_{\mathrm{on}}=\tilde E_{\mathrm{off}}$;
cycle-charging raises $T^{\mathrm{ge}}$ and widens the band; a combined policy selects
the cheaper of the two each step; and predictive control corresponds to $L>0$. The class
$\Theta$ is constructed so that every $\pi_\theta$ produces trajectories in the feasible
set $\mathcal{F}(x)$---it never charges and discharges simultaneously, respects the
minimum loading $F^{\mathrm{ge}}$ and the state-of-charge trips---so
Assumption~\ref{ass:feas} holds for all $\theta\in\Theta$.

\paragraph{The bound is invariant to the controller.} This last point has a decisive
consequence. By Proposition~\ref{prop:bound}, $C^{\mathrm{opt}}_n(x)\le
C^{\mathrm{rule}}_n(x;\theta)$ for \emph{every} feasible $\theta$, because each $\pi_\theta$
is one feasible dispatch. Hence the lower bound $z_A^\star$ and the entire
branch-and-simulate certificate of Section~\ref{sec:certify} are \emph{independent of the
choice of controller}: refining the rules can only \emph{lower} the upper bound
$z_B(\theta)$, never invalidate the certificate. Controller refinement is therefore
``free'' with respect to correctness---a better rule simply shrinks the certified price of
the heuristic dispatch.

\paragraph{Gap-minimising tuning.} The best controller in the class, for a given sizing
$x$, minimises the expected discounted operating cost,
\begin{equation}
  \theta^\star(x) \;=\; \arg\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\;
  \sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}} P_n\,B_n\,\Delta_n\,C^{\mathrm{rule}}_n(x;\theta),
  \label{eq:tune}
\end{equation}
which, since $C^{\mathrm{rule}}$ is evaluated by forward simulation, is solved by a
derivative-free search over the low-dimensional $\Theta$ (grid, Nelder--Mead or
differential evolution). Crucially, the cost-optimal dispatch acts as a \emph{teacher}:
comparing the optimal trajectory $u^{\mathrm{opt}}_n(x)$ with the trajectory of $\pi_\theta$
exposes the exact time steps at which the rule loses money, which both warm-starts $\theta$
(imitation of the optimal policy) and guides the choice of which parameters to enrich.
Substituting $\theta^\star(x)$ into the upper-bound oracle of branch-and-simulate tightens
$z_B^\star$ and, jointly with the sizing search, co-selects the pair $(x,\theta)$.

\paragraph{Achievable price and its decomposition.} For a policy class $\Theta$ define the
class-residual price
$\Delta_{\mathrm{heur}}(\Theta)=\min_{\theta\in\Theta} z_B(\theta)-z_A^\star\ge0$, which is
non-increasing as $\Theta$ is enriched and tends to zero as the class approaches the
full-foresight cost-optimal policy. It answers, quantitatively and with a certificate, the
practical question \emph{``how close to optimal can a simple deployable rule get?''}
Writing $\Theta_{\mathrm{c}}$ for the causal (finite-foresight) sub-class, the price splits
as
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_{\mathrm{heur}}(\theta)
  = \underbrace{\big[z_B(\theta)-\min_{\vartheta\in\Theta_{\mathrm{c}}}z_B(\vartheta)\big]}
      _{\text{sub-optimality of the chosen rule}}
  + \underbrace{\big[\min_{\vartheta\in\Theta_{\mathrm{c}}}z_B(\vartheta)-z_A^\star\big]}
      _{\text{value of foresight}} ,
\end{equation}
separating the cost of using a simple rule (attacked by the hysteresis, setpoint and
reserve parameters) from the cost of imperfect information (attacked by the look-ahead
horizon $L$). The decomposition tells the planner whether to invest in smarter logic or in
better forecasting.

\paragraph{A forecasting controller is a member of the class.} A control module now in
development is an instance of this class. Running beside the plant, it forecasts demand and
yield over the coming hours and sends the inverters the state of charge at which the
generator must start: it sets the start threshold $\tilde E_{\mathrm{on}}$ causally, from a
forecast over a horizon $L$, and leaves the dispatch itself to the inverters. Provided the
inverters go on enforcing the trip limits and the minimum loading, as they do under the
present rule, its trajectories remain in $\mathcal{F}(x)$, the certificate holds for it
unchanged, and its value is measured as the reduction of $z_B(\theta)$ it achieves at a
fixed sizing---a share of the price of the heuristic dispatch recovered by a parameter sent
to the controller rather than by capacity.

\subsection{Relation to prior work}

The literature on off-grid microgrid sizing separates into two families that this
construction bridges. The first uses rule-based controllers---HOMER's load-following,
cycle-charging, generator-order and combined strategies, and the genetic-algorithm tool
iHOGA/MHOGA of Dufo-L\'opez and Bernal-Agust\'in---but searches the capacity space by
enumeration or metaheuristics and reports \emph{no} optimality certificate or gap. The
second uses exact mathematical programming---e.g.\ the MILP planning model of Wu \emph{et
al.}\ (IEEE Trans.\ Smart Grid 12(5), 2021), the MicroGridsPy capacity-expansion framework
of Stevanato \emph{et al.}, and the multi-stage stochastic integer template of Ahmed,
King \& Parija (J.\ Glob.\ Optim.\ 26, 2003)---which certifies optimality but only for
\emph{cost-optimal} dispatch. The closest hybrids confirm rather than pre-empt the gap:
the inner--outer co-optimisation of Lyu \emph{et al.}\ (Applied Energy 363, 2024) keeps an
optimiser in the lower level and explicitly deems sizing by rule-based strategies
``risky''; the progressive-hedging scheme of Zolan/Wales \emph{et al.}\ (Optimization and
Engineering 26, 2025) yields an optimality gap but bounds the cost-optimal-dispatch MILP
itself; and the rule-based controller tuning of Nespoli \& Medici (arXiv:2511.21619, 2025)
uses a metaheuristic with no certificate and explicitly dismisses a nested
master/inner-optimisation as too costly. To our knowledge, no prior work certifies global
optimality of the sizing \emph{for a fixed rule-based controller} by using cost-optimal
dispatch as a guaranteed lower bound, nor quantifies the price of the heuristic dispatch as
a certified gap. Two qualifications are in order: the certificate rests on
Assumption~\ref{ass:feas}, which must be verified for any controller to which the method is
applied, and the extension of the bound to a genuinely adaptive multi-stage recourse---in
which a fixed (non-optimising) policy would remove the integer recourse that obstructs
stochastic dual dynamic integer programming---is a promising but as yet unproven direction.
